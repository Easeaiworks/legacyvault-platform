Most people spend a lot of energy thinking about what happens after they die. Far fewer think about what happens if they *don't* die — but become unable to make financial decisions.

A durable financial power of attorney (sometimes called a **DPOA** or **financial POA**) is the document that solves the second problem. And it's arguably more important than your will, because the situation it protects against is one you might survive for years.

## What it actually is

A DPOA is a legal document that appoints someone — called your **agent** (or *attorney-in-fact*, confusingly) — to manage your financial affairs if you become mentally or physically unable to manage them yourself.

The word **"durable"** matters. A non-durable POA only works while you have mental capacity. A durable POA continues to work after you lose capacity — which is exactly when it's needed. Every state has its own version, but the durable one is what you want.

## What it covers

- **Banking:** accessing your accounts, paying bills, managing your mortgage
- **Investments:** managing your portfolio, making buy/sell decisions, working with your financial advisor
- **Real estate:** selling or refinancing property if needed to fund your care
- **Taxes:** filing returns, dealing with the IRS
- **Insurance claims:** filing and managing health, disability, and long-term care claims
- **Government benefits:** applying for or managing Social Security, Medicare, veterans' benefits
- **Business:** managing your business interests, signing contracts

You can grant broad authority ("everything") or narrow it down to specific powers. Most people use broad durable POAs so their agent isn't hitting walls at the moment they most need to move.

## Springing vs. immediate — a real decision

There are two flavors of durable POA:

**Immediate** — takes effect the moment it's signed. Your agent could technically use it while you're still perfectly capable of managing your own affairs. Most people who name a trusted spouse or adult child are comfortable with this; the agent simply won't use it until needed.

**Springing** — only takes effect when a specified condition is met, usually a doctor's written declaration of incapacity. Sounds safer, is often more of a headache. Getting a doctor to sign a formal incapacity declaration is harder than most people expect, and banks and financial institutions often push back on springing POAs because they can't independently verify the trigger. If you use a springing POA, get the specific triggering language reviewed by an attorney familiar with your state.

Our recommendation for most families: **immediate**, given to a spouse or trusted family member you'd trust with your finances anyway.

## The court alternative — conservatorship

Without a durable POA, your family's only path to legal authority over your finances is a court-supervised **conservatorship** (in some states, **guardianship of the estate**).

This is not fast. It's not cheap. It's not private. And once it's set up, your conservator has to file annual accountings with the court, get court permission for major decisions, and pay ongoing legal fees for as long as the arrangement lasts.

Typical costs:
- Initial petition and hearing: **$5,000–$15,000** in legal fees
- Bond premium (often required): **$500–$2,000/year**
- Annual accounting and court fees: **$2,000–$5,000/year**
- Contested conservatorships (where family members disagree about who should serve): **easily $50,000+**

Compare to the $200–$500 cost of a durable POA prepared in advance.

## Who to name — and the second choice matters

You need to think about two people, not one.

**Your primary agent** should be someone you trust completely with your money, who lives close enough to actually help, who is organized enough to keep records, and who is younger or healthier than you (so they'll be around when needed). Most people pick a spouse first.

**Your successor agent** takes over if your primary can't serve — because they've died, moved away, gotten sick themselves, or declined the role. Naming a successor is not optional; without one, if your primary can't serve, your POA effectively fails.

## What POAs *don't* cover

A financial POA covers financial decisions. It does **not** cover healthcare decisions — you need a separate **healthcare power of attorney** (sometimes called a healthcare proxy or medical POA) for those. Never assume one document covers both. Most estate planning packages include both, but if yours doesn't, that's a gap.

## What to do this week

If you don't have a durable financial POA, this is the second-most important estate planning document in your life — arguably more important than your will if you're at any material risk of stroke, cognitive decline, or serious accident. Prices are low. The alternative is punishing for your family.

If you already have one, check the date. POAs older than 10 years are sometimes rejected by financial institutions on their face, even where they're technically still valid. A refresh every 5 years is a good idea.
