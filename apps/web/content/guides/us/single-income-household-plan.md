Single-income households — where one adult earns and the other manages the home, cares for children, or is currently unable to work — face a distinct estate-planning challenge. The family's financial survival depends on protecting exactly one paycheck.

Most of the standard advice ("save more," "diversify," "delay Social Security") applies to you too. But there are five things you have to get right that dual-income families can afford to be less careful about.

## 1. Life insurance sized to the actual replacement cost

Group life insurance through work — usually 1–2× salary — is nowhere near enough for a single-income household.

**Rough sizing:** aim for **10–12× the earner's annual income** in term life insurance. For a $100,000 earner, that's $1M–$1.2M of coverage. Sounds like a lot; it isn't, once you look at what it actually replaces.

- Mortgage payoff: often $200,000–$500,000 for a family home
- Kids to age 22 (living expenses + education): $250,000–$500,000
- Spouse's income replacement (5–10 years until they can retrain and re-enter the workforce): $250,000–$500,000
- Funeral, taxes, immediate emergency reserves: $30,000–$60,000

**The good news:** term life insurance is cheap. A healthy 35-year-old male non-smoker can get $1M of 20-year term for **$40–$70 per month**. Women pay less. Whole life / permanent policies cost 5–10× as much for the same coverage — for most single-income families, term is the right answer.

## 2. Disability insurance is arguably more important

Statistically, the single earner is much more likely to become disabled than to die during working years. About **1 in 4 workers** will experience a disability lasting more than 90 days at some point in their career.

Social Security Disability (SSDI) is real but hard to qualify for, slow to approve, and pays modestly. If your household depends on one paycheck, private long-term disability insurance is essential.

**Rough sizing:** enough to replace **60–70% of the earner's after-tax income** to age 65. A short-term policy (3–6 months of coverage) is often included in employer benefits; a long-term policy usually isn't and needs to be purchased separately, either through work at a discount or independently. Cost is typically **1%–3% of income covered**.

## 3. Both spouses on the accounts

In a single-income household, the non-earner is often not named on the family's financial accounts. This creates real problems in an emergency:

- If the earning spouse becomes incapacitated, the non-earner can't access the money
- If the earning spouse dies, everything is frozen pending probate
- If the household's credit cards are all in the earning spouse's name, the non-earning spouse has no independent credit history — a serious problem if they need to re-enter the workforce or apply for a mortgage on their own

**Concrete actions:**

- Add the non-earning spouse as a **joint owner** on at least one checking account, one savings account, and one credit card
- Consider adding the non-earning spouse as an authorized signer on other accounts even if not a joint owner
- Make sure the non-earning spouse has their own credit history — a credit card in their name only, used and paid off, is enough

## 4. Retirement savings for the non-earning spouse

The IRS lets a non-earning spouse contribute to an IRA using the working spouse's income (subject to household income limits) — a **spousal IRA**. In 2026 the limits are the same: $7,000/year ($8,000 if 50+).

This is important for two reasons:

1. It builds retirement wealth in the non-earning spouse's name — protecting them if the marriage ends and giving them independent retirement assets.
2. Roth spousal IRAs grow tax-free forever, including for beneficiaries.

Most single-income households don't max the spousal IRA and don't realize they could.

## 5. The beneficiary audit

Every account with a beneficiary designation should name the current spouse first, contingent beneficiaries second, and never "my estate."

The classic disaster (see the scenario above): the working spouse changed jobs, rolled over a 401(k) to an IRA, and never updated the beneficiary on the *old* account. Or married and never changed the designation from their previous partner or parent. Or divorced and remarried and only updated the will.

**Beneficiary designations override your will.** Every time. If your ex-spouse or your mother is on file at Vanguard as the beneficiary of your 401(k), that's who gets the money — not the person named in your current will.

Do the audit today. Log into every account (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA, life insurance, pension, HSA, brokerage TOD). Look at who's listed. Update anything that's wrong.

## The order of operations

If you're starting from zero:

1. **Term life insurance** — the biggest single lift for the least money
2. **Employer's group disability** if available; supplement if needed
3. **Beneficiary audit** — 30 minutes of your life
4. **Emergency fund** — build to 3 months of expenses fast, then 6 months
5. **Non-earning spouse on accounts** — joint checking, joint credit card, independent credit
6. **Spousal IRA** — start contributing now, even if only $100/month
7. **Will + POA + healthcare directive** — the essentials (see our other guides)

If you do steps 1–3 this week, you've meaningfully reduced the single largest financial risk your family faces.
