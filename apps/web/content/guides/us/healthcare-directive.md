If you're in a serious accident and can't speak for yourself, someone else is going to make medical decisions on your behalf. The only real question is: is it someone you chose, working from instructions you gave — or is it a doctor, a court-appointed guardian, or a family member who's disagreeing with another family member?

The paperwork to answer that question is straightforward, cheap, and one of the highest-value hours you'll ever spend.

## Two documents, one purpose

In most US states there are two related but distinct documents:

**Healthcare Power of Attorney** (sometimes called a **healthcare proxy**, **medical POA**, or **durable POA for healthcare**) — appoints a person to make medical decisions on your behalf if you can't. This person is often called your **healthcare agent** or **surrogate**.

**Living Will** (sometimes called an **advance directive** or **advance healthcare directive**) — spells out your specific wishes about end-of-life care: whether you want to be kept on life support, whether you want a feeding tube, whether you want CPR, whether you want palliative care in your final days.

Some states combine these into a single document called an **Advance Healthcare Directive**. Others keep them separate. Some states have their own name entirely (California uses "Advance Health Care Directive"; New York uses "Health Care Proxy" for the appointment part plus a separate living will).

For most people, you want **both**. The healthcare POA covers all the situations you couldn't have predicted; the living will handles the specific end-of-life scenarios where you have strong preferences.

## What decisions are covered

- Consent to or refusal of medical treatment, surgery, medication, diagnostic tests
- End-of-life care: DNR orders, withdrawal of life support, palliative care preferences
- Living arrangements if you can't return home: which facility, when to move
- Personal-care decisions if you can't make them: hygiene, clothing, nutrition
- Organ and tissue donation preferences
- Autopsy and disposition of remains

Some states let you include specific instructions about mental health treatment. If you have particular views about psychiatric medication or involuntary commitment, ask your attorney about whether your state's form covers those.

## Who to name

Pick one person as your primary healthcare agent. Also name a successor.

The best healthcare agent is someone who:

- Knows you well enough to guess what you'd want in a situation you didn't spell out
- Can be reached quickly (has a working phone, isn't traveling constantly)
- Is willing to make hard decisions and stand by them under family pressure
- Isn't easily bullied by other family members who may disagree
- Is emotionally capable of being there — some people simply cannot handle it, and it's better to know that in advance

The best healthcare agent is often not the same person as your financial POA. The skills are different. It's fine — often preferable — to name different people for each.

## The HIPAA piece — often overlooked

Even with a healthcare POA, hospitals sometimes refuse to release information to your agent because of HIPAA (the federal medical privacy law). Modern healthcare POA forms usually include an explicit HIPAA release, but if yours is old, it may not.

A short standalone **HIPAA authorization** naming your healthcare agent as an authorized recipient of your medical information solves this. It's not a substitute for the POA, but it complements it — some hospitals will accept the HIPAA release faster than the POA.

## State-by-state notes

- **California, New York, Texas, Florida, Illinois** each have their own state-specific forms. Using the state form (available free from the state department of health) is often the cleanest path.
- **Five Wishes** is a widely accepted advance directive document that meets requirements in most states (all but a few) and covers more emotional/personal preferences than the standard state forms.
- **POLST / MOLST** forms (Physician / Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) are a separate document, usually for people with serious illness who want physician orders about end-of-life care. Different from a living will — they're actual medical orders, not just wishes. Ask your doctor.

## What to do this week

The free state-department-of-health forms are legally valid and take about 20 minutes to complete. You need two witnesses (in most states) or a notary. That's the entire barrier.

If you have a complex situation — you're in a blended family with strong disagreements, you have specific religious preferences, you have a rare medical condition — pay a lawyer $200–$400 to draft custom directives. For most other people, the state form is enough.

**Once you have signed directives, make sure your primary care doctor has a copy, your healthcare agent has a copy, and a copy is in your vault** — because a signed directive nobody can find is functionally the same as no directive at all.
