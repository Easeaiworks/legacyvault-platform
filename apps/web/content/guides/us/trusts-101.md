A trust is a container. You put assets into it. You name someone (a *trustee*) to manage what's inside, and you write instructions for how it's managed and who eventually gets what. It's a legal fiction, but a powerful one — because the container itself owns the assets, not you.

## Why the container matters

When you die, everything titled in your personal name goes through probate — a public court proceeding. Everything titled in a trust does not. That's the entire pitch for a revocable living trust: **privacy, speed, and cost avoidance at death**.

For estates that own real estate in expensive-probate states (California, Florida, New York, Massachusetts), a revocable living trust often saves the estate multiples of its setup cost.

## The five trusts you'll hear about

**Revocable living trust.** The most common. You create it while alive, you can change or dissolve it at any time, and you typically name yourself as the initial trustee. At death, a successor trustee takes over and distributes assets according to your instructions — no court involvement. Does NOT save estate tax and does NOT protect from creditors (because you still control the assets). But it does bypass probate.

**Irrevocable trust.** You give up control. In exchange, the assets are no longer legally yours — which can protect from creditors and reduce estate tax exposure. Used for high-net-worth planning, life insurance ownership (ILITs), and Medicaid asset protection (with a five-year lookback in most states).

**Testamentary trust.** Created inside your will, funded at your death. Used most commonly to hold inheritances for minor children until an age you specify (25, 30, staged distributions). Does NOT avoid probate — because it's part of your will, it goes through probate first — but it does protect the inheritance itself from being handed to an 18-year-old.

**Special needs trust.** Holds assets for a disabled beneficiary without disqualifying them from means-tested government benefits (SSI, Medicaid). Highly technical — requires an attorney who specializes.

**Spendthrift / dynasty / QTIP / marital / bypass trusts.** Specialized tools for creditor protection, multi-generational transfer, second marriages, and estate tax planning at higher wealth levels. If your estate is under $5M and your life is straightforward, you likely don't need these.

## When you probably don't need a trust

- Your estate is small and mostly held in joint accounts, TOD/POD accounts, and beneficiary-designated retirement plans (all of which already bypass probate).
- You live in a state with a simplified probate process for small estates (many states exempt estates under $50k–$150k).
- Your family situation is simple — a spouse who inherits everything, or adult children with no special needs and no likely conflicts.

## When a trust is probably worth it

- You own real estate — especially in California, Florida, New York, or another expensive-probate state.
- You want privacy about who inherits and how much (a will becomes public record; a trust does not).
- You have minor children and want inheritances managed until they're mature.
- You have a beneficiary with special needs, substance abuse issues, or a shaky marriage.
- You're in a second marriage and want to provide for your current spouse *and* preserve assets for children from a first marriage.
- Your estate is over the federal estate tax threshold ($13.61M in 2024, sunsetting to ~$7M in 2026).

## The most important warning

**Setting up a trust is not the same as funding it.** You have to re-title every asset you want in the trust — deeds, brokerage accounts, business interests. Attorneys write the trust, but they don't automatically retitle your accounts. If you don't do the funding, the trust is an expensive piece of paper with nothing in it, and probate happens anyway.

Set up the trust. Then spend a Saturday transferring titles.
