Every state has an answer to the question *"what happens if this person dies without leaving instructions?"* — and none of those answers look like what most people actually want.

## What a will actually does

A last will and testament is a legal document that spells out three things:

**Who gets what.** Every asset you own that isn't otherwise directed (by beneficiary designation on a 401(k), by joint ownership on a house, by trust) is distributed according to your will.

**Who's in charge.** You name an **executor** (called a *personal representative* in some states) — the person legally responsible for gathering your assets, paying your debts and taxes, and distributing what remains.

**Who raises your kids.** If you have minor children, your will names a **guardian**. Without one, a judge picks — and the judge may not pick who you would.

That third one is the piece that shocks people. A guardian named in a will can be challenged, but the will's designation carries enormous weight. A guardian assigned by a court with no guidance can be someone you would never have chosen.

## The four traps we see most often

**Marriage or divorce didn't automatically update your will.** In most states, marriage revokes any pre-existing will unless the will specifically anticipates it. Divorce revokes provisions naming your ex-spouse — in some states. In others it doesn't. This is one of the most common sources of unintended inheritance.

**Your will doesn't cover beneficiary-designated assets.** Life insurance, retirement accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA), pensions, transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts — these all pass to whoever is listed as the beneficiary. Your will can't override that. If your ex-spouse is still listed on your IRA, they get the IRA. Full stop.

**Handwritten (holographic) wills are risky.** Some states accept them, most don't, and even where accepted they're routinely challenged. If you write a will yourself, use a properly witnessed and notarized document.

**No update after major life events.** Marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary, significant change in assets, moving to a different state — any of these can make your will inadequate.

## What actually costs money

The comparison isn't "cost of a will vs. free." It's "cost of a will vs. cost of not having one."

A properly drafted will costs $500–$2,500 through an attorney, or $50–$300 through an online service for a straightforward situation. Getting probate wrong can cost $10,000–$50,000+ in legal fees, delays, and family disputes — and that number climbs fast for contested estates or blended families.

## State-by-state variation matters

A will drafted in Illinois has to comply with Illinois execution requirements (usually two disinterested witnesses, signed in each other's presence). A will drafted in Louisiana follows French civil-law rules that are quite different. Moving states after signing your will means it's worth having it reviewed for continued validity in your new state.

Community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin) treat marital property differently than common-law states. If you live in one and your spouse dies without a will, half the community property already belongs to you — the intestacy formula only distributes the deceased's half. In common-law states, the entire estate goes through intestacy.

## What to do this week

If you don't have a will, this is the highest-leverage document in your life for the amount of time it takes. A basic will for a straightforward situation can be done in an evening. A more complex situation — business ownership, blended family, disabled dependent, significant assets — needs an attorney.

Either way: **do it before your next birthday.**
