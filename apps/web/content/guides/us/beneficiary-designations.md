The will you spent $2,000 drafting doesn't touch the biggest assets you own. That's not a bug — it's how the law works, and almost nobody knows.

## What beneficiary designations override

Any account with a named beneficiary passes directly to that person the moment you die. Your will has no authority over it. That means:

- **401(k), 403(b), 457, IRA, Roth IRA** — federal law (ERISA for employer plans) makes the beneficiary designation controlling.
- **Life insurance policies** — the contract with the insurer controls, not your will.
- **Pension survivor benefits** — the plan document controls.
- **Transfer-on-death (TOD) brokerage accounts** — the TOD form controls.
- **Payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts** — the POD form controls.
- **Annuities** — the annuity contract controls.
- **Health Savings Accounts (HSAs)** — the custodian's beneficiary form controls.

For most Americans, these accounts hold more wealth than everything else combined. And they all pass outside your will.

## The four rules almost nobody knows

**Rule 1: Marriage does not automatically update your beneficiaries.** Getting married doesn't remove your ex — or your parents, or a former partner — from any form. Every account needs to be updated by hand.

**Rule 2: Divorce might revoke a designation. Or might not.** ERISA plans (most employer 401(k)s) are governed by federal law and generally do NOT auto-revoke on divorce — even if your state has a revocation-on-divorce statute. This is the single most common source of "the ex got everything." IRAs are governed by state law and results vary.

**Rule 3: Naming your estate is almost always the wrong answer.** If you leave the beneficiary blank or write "my estate," the retirement account goes through probate and loses the stretched-distribution rules that let heirs (especially spouses) minimize tax. Name a person.

**Rule 4: Naming a minor as beneficiary triggers a court proceeding.** If a 10-year-old inherits an IRA directly, a court appoints a conservator to manage it until the child turns 18. Use a trust or a UTMA custodianship instead.

## What to actually do

Set a recurring calendar reminder — once a year, and after any major life event — to do the following:

1. Pull the beneficiary designation on every account listed above.
2. Confirm both a **primary** and a **contingent** beneficiary are named.
3. If you're married, name your spouse as primary unless there's a specific reason not to (and if not, get legal advice — spousal consent may be required for non-spouse beneficiaries on 401(k)s).
4. For beneficiaries who are minors, disabled, or financially vulnerable, talk to an estate attorney about using a trust as the named beneficiary rather than the person directly.
5. Store a copy of every completed form with your estate documents. Plan administrators sometimes lose them.

Twenty minutes a year, once a year. It's the highest-leverage thing you can do for the people you love.
