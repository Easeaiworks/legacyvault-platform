The industry rule of thumb — **10–12× your annual income** — is a decent starting point but leaves too much to chance for most families. The DIME method is the standard framework that estate lawyers, financial planners, and honest insurance advisors use to size coverage properly.

## DIME

**D — Debt.** Everything you owe that would need to be paid off or serviced from your estate:
- Mortgage balance
- Auto loans
- Student loans (usually discharged at death if federal; private loans vary)
- Credit card and other consumer debt
- Business debts you've personally guaranteed

Add these up. That's your D.

**I — Income.** Multiply your annual net income by how many years your family would need to replace it. For someone with young children, this is usually **10–15 years** — enough time for the surviving spouse to establish a career (or reestablish one) and for kids to reach age of independence.

Formula: (annual after-tax income) × (years of replacement) = your I.

**M — Mortgage.** If you counted mortgage under D, don't double-count here. Some versions of DIME break mortgage out separately to force families to think about whether they want the death benefit to pay it off outright (recommended in most cases) or continue paying it monthly.

**E — Education.** Estimated cost of the children's post-secondary education:
- Public 4-year in-state at today's prices: $100,000–$130,000 per child (tuition + room + board)
- Private 4-year: $250,000–$350,000 per child
- If you're planning ahead 15–18 years, inflate at 5% annually

## A real calculation

For our marketing director from the scenario:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| **Debt** (mortgage $520k, car loan $18k, credit cards $6k) | $544,000 |
| **Income** ($120k after-tax × 12 years) | $1,440,000 |
| **Mortgage** (included above) | $0 |
| **Education** ($130k × 3 kids in state schools) | $390,000 |
| Subtract existing coverage & liquid assets | −$400,000 |
| **Recommended coverage** | **$1,974,000** |

You'd round up to $2M in a single policy or split across two policies for underwriting flexibility. $2M of 20-year term for a healthy 39-year-old non-smoker runs about **$70–$110 per month**.

## Term vs. permanent

**Term life** covers you for a specified period (10, 20, 30 years) and pays out only if you die during the term. It's the right answer for **90%+ of families**. Cheap, straightforward, sized for the years of highest financial risk (mortgage, young kids, peak earning years). When the term expires, you don't have coverage — but by then the mortgage is paid off and the kids are grown.

**Whole life / permanent life** covers you for your entire life and includes a savings component. It costs **5–10× as much** as equivalent term coverage. Appropriate in narrow situations:

- Estate liquidity for high-net-worth families (federal estate tax on estates over ~$14M in 2026)
- Equalizing inheritances when a family business goes to one child
- Long-term charitable giving with a policy structure
- Certain business succession scenarios

If someone is pitching you whole life without one of the above being your actual situation, get a second opinion from a fee-only advisor.

## Length of term

**20-year term** is the most common and usually the right answer for parents of young children. It gets you through mortgage payoff and kids-to-adulthood.

**30-year term** costs a bit more but takes you further. Worth it if you're starting a family later, or if you want your family covered through the highest-earning years.

**10-year term** is cheap now but you'll be older when it expires — and re-underwriting at 45 or 50 costs more than the extra $10/month you saved.

## Getting the right price

Life insurance pricing is entirely driven by underwriting: age, sex, smoking status, health, family history, and occupation. The published rates you see on comparison sites are for **preferred plus** — the top health tier. Most people don't qualify. A minor issue (elevated cholesterol, mild hypertension, a family history of heart disease) can move you a tier or two down, meaning 20–50% higher premiums.

**Use an independent broker,** not a captive agent from one company. An independent broker shops your health profile across 20+ carriers and finds the one that underwrites your specific situation most favorably. This is free to you — brokers are paid by the insurance company — and can save you 30–50% versus the first quote.

**Get quotes from multiple carriers.** Life insurance is a commodity. There is no "brand name" advantage worth paying for.

## When to reevaluate

- **Every major life event:** marriage, birth of a child, home purchase, divorce, business ownership change.
- **Every 5 years otherwise.** As your net worth grows and your mortgage shrinks, your coverage need may decrease.
- **Before the term expires.** If your original 20-year term is running out and your kids are 10 and 12, you probably need to renew (at your then-current age, so it'll cost more).

## What to do this week

If you have no life insurance and dependents, get 3 quotes for the DIME-recommended amount from an independent broker. Apply for the best fit — the application process takes 4–8 weeks and includes a medical exam. Don't wait for the "perfect" moment. The premium you pay at 40 is meaningfully higher than what you'd pay at 35.

If you already have coverage, log it into your vault so your family knows it exists, and check that:

- The beneficiary is current (not your ex-spouse from 15 years ago)
- The contingent beneficiary is named (in case the primary predeceases you)
- The amount still reflects your family's actual need
