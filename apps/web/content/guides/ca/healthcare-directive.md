If you're in a serious accident and can't speak for yourself, someone else is going to make medical decisions for you. The only real question is: is it someone you chose, working from instructions you gave — or is it a doctor, a court-appointed guardian, or a family member disagreeing with another family member?

The paperwork to answer that question is straightforward, cheap, and one of the highest-value hours you'll ever spend.

## Terminology varies by province

Every province has slightly different language for essentially the same document:

- **Ontario:** Power of Attorney for Personal Care (Substitute Decisions Act)
- **Alberta:** Personal Directive
- **British Columbia:** Representation Agreement (Section 9 covers most healthcare decisions; Section 7 is a lighter form)
- **Saskatchewan:** Health Care Directive
- **Manitoba:** Health Care Directive
- **Quebec:** Mandate given in anticipation of incapacity (covers both financial and personal care)
- **Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, PEI, Newfoundland:** each has its own equivalent

The name changes; the concept doesn't. You're appointing someone (or setting out written wishes) to guide medical and personal-care decisions if you can't make them yourself.

## What's covered

- Medical decisions: consent to or refusal of treatment, surgery, medication, tests
- End-of-life care: DNR, withdrawal of life support, palliative care preferences
- Living arrangements: which long-term care facility, when to move
- Personal care: hygiene, clothing, nutrition, recreation
- Organ and tissue donation preferences

Some provinces also allow specific written wishes about mental-health treatment. If you have views on psychiatric medication or involuntary hospitalization, check whether your provincial form covers those.

## Substitute decision-makers by default — and why that's not enough

Every province has a **default hierarchy** for who makes decisions if you haven't appointed anyone. Ontario's Health Care Consent Act, for example, ranks: spouse → adult child → parent → sibling → any other relative.

Two problems with relying on the default:

**"Spouse" is ambiguous in blended families.** Common-law partner? Ex-spouse who you're still on paper married to? The person you've been living with for 15 years but never legally married? Provincial rules vary and courts get involved.

**Family disputes go to the courts.** If your adult children disagree with each other or with your spouse about what treatment you'd want, the hospital can't just pick one. A court has to decide. That takes time — time during which you're getting treatment (or not) that doesn't match your wishes.

A signed directive eliminates the ambiguity. It names one specific person and, ideally, writes down what you want.

## Who to name

Pick one person as your primary attorney/representative. Also name an alternate.

The best person to appoint:

- Knows you well enough to guess what you'd want in situations you didn't spell out
- Can be reached quickly
- Is willing to make hard decisions and stand by them under family pressure
- Isn't easily bullied by other family members who may disagree
- Is emotionally capable of being there — some people simply cannot handle it

Often not the same person as your POA for property. The skills are different. Fine to name different people for each.

## Written wishes vs. discretionary authority

You have a choice about how much to write down.

**Discretionary approach:** appoint someone you trust completely and say very little about specific preferences. Let them decide based on their knowledge of you. Works well when you deeply trust your person and don't have strong views about specific scenarios.

**Detailed approach:** write down specific preferences — no ventilator beyond X weeks, no feeding tube in end-stage dementia, DNR under specified conditions. Works well when you have strong preferences or your appointed person needs backup for the difficult calls.

Most people benefit from something in between: appoint a strong primary, add a paragraph of written wishes covering the two or three scenarios you feel strongly about. Blank templates in every province include prompts for this.

## Quebec's Mandate is different

Quebec's civil-law regime uses a single **mandat de protection** ("mandate given in anticipation of incapacity") that covers both financial and personal-care decisions. It must be prepared by a notary or witnessed carefully according to provincial requirements. Once you become incapacitated, the mandate has to be **homologated** by a court before taking effect — an additional step but with court oversight built in.

If you live in Quebec, work with a notary. Don't download a template.

## What to do this week

Free provincial forms exist in most provinces (Ontario, Alberta, BC, Manitoba, Saskatchewan all publish templates). Filling one out takes about 30 minutes; witnessing takes another 15.

If your situation is complex — blended family with disagreements, strong religious preferences, unusual medical history — pay a lawyer $200–$400 to draft a custom directive.

**Once signed, make sure your family doctor has a copy, your appointed person has a copy, and a copy is in your vault.** A directive nobody can find is functionally the same as no directive at all.
