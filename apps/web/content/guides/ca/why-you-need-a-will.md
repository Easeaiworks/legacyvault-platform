Every province has an answer to *"what happens if this person dies without leaving instructions?"* — and in most of Canada, that answer is far more punishing for common-law couples than most people realize.

## What a will actually does

A last will and testament is a legal document that spells out three things:

**Who gets what.** Every asset you own that isn't otherwise directed (by beneficiary designation on an RRSP or TFSA, by joint ownership with right of survivorship, or by trust) is distributed according to your will.

**Who's in charge.** You name an **estate trustee** (Ontario), **executor** (most provinces), or **liquidator** (Quebec) — the person legally responsible for gathering your assets, paying your debts and taxes, and distributing what remains.

**Who raises your kids.** If you have minor children, your will names a **guardian**. Without one, a court decides — and it may not be who you would have chosen.

## The common-law trap

This is the single biggest reason to have a will in Canada if you're not legally married.

In **most provinces (Ontario, Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Nova Scotia, PEI, New Brunswick, Newfoundland)**, common-law partners have *no rights under intestacy law*. If you die without a will, your common-law partner of 30 years inherits nothing from your estate. Your assets go to your legal next-of-kin under the provincial formula — parents, siblings, adult children from prior relationships — none of whom may be who you would have chosen.

**British Columbia** is an important exception: under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act, common-law partners who have lived together for 2+ years are treated the same as married spouses under intestacy. Quebec's civil-law regime has its own rules and doesn't recognize common-law inheritance rights at all — a notarial will is essentially required.

If you're common-law anywhere except BC, and you don't have a will, your partner is not protected.

## The four other traps we see most often

**Marriage automatically revokes your will in most provinces.** Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, and PEI have removed this rule — but Ontario (as of 2022), BC, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Newfoundland still automatically revoke wills on marriage unless the will specifically anticipates it.

**Divorce doesn't automatically revoke provisions naming your ex-spouse in all provinces.** In some, an ex-spouse can still inherit under an unrevised will.

**Beneficiary-designated assets pass outside your will.** RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs, life insurance, pensions — these all pass to whoever is listed as the beneficiary, regardless of what your will says. If your ex-spouse is still listed on your RRSP, they get the RRSP. Full stop.

**Handwritten (holographic) wills are only valid in some provinces.** Alberta, Ontario, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, and New Brunswick accept them. BC, Nova Scotia, and PEI do not. Even where valid, they're routinely challenged.

## What it actually costs — a real comparison

**Ontario probate fees on a $500,000 estate** are **$6,750** (Estate Administration Tax at 1.5% of value over $50,000). Add legal fees of $5,000–$15,000 for typical estate administration. Total immediate cost before family sees a dollar: $11,750–$21,750. This is *with* a will and normal probate.

**Without a will**, add roughly $5,000–$15,000 in additional legal costs, months of delay, and often family conflict that never fully repairs.

**A properly drafted will** costs $500–$2,500 through an estate lawyer, or $99–$300 through Canadian online services like Willful or Epilogue for straightforward situations.

## Province-by-province highlights

**Ontario** probate fees are 1.5% on everything above $50,000 — the second-highest in Canada. Probate avoidance strategies (multiple wills, alter ego trusts, joint ownership) are worth serious consideration for larger estates.

**Alberta** caps probate fees at approximately $525 regardless of estate size. Probate avoidance is much less important; a well-drafted will suffices for most estates.

**British Columbia** charges roughly 1.4% of estate value. Similar dynamics to Ontario — probate avoidance matters.

**Quebec** notarial wills bypass probate entirely. Notarial wills are prepared and stored by a notary; a holograph or witnessed will still requires probate. For most Quebec residents, the notarial will is standard.

## What to do this week

If you don't have a will, this is the highest-leverage document in your life for the amount of time it takes. A basic will for a straightforward situation can be done in an evening with an online service or in a couple of meetings with a lawyer.

**Do it before your next birthday** — especially if you're common-law and don't live in BC.
