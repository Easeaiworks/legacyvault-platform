Most people spend a lot of energy thinking about what happens after they die. Far fewer think about what happens if they *don't* die — but become unable to make financial decisions.

A **Continuing** (Ontario) or **Enduring** (Alberta, BC, most other provinces) Power of Attorney for Property is the document that solves the second problem. Arguably it's more important than your will, because the situation it protects against is one you might survive for years.

## What it actually is

A POA for property appoints someone — called your **attorney** (confusingly, not a lawyer) — to manage your financial affairs if you become mentally or physically unable to manage them yourself.

The words **"continuing"** or **"enduring"** matter. A **General** POA only works while you have mental capacity. A **Continuing/Enduring** POA continues to work after you lose capacity — which is exactly when it's needed. Every province has slightly different terminology, but the concept is the same.

- **Ontario:** Continuing Power of Attorney for Property (under the Substitute Decisions Act)
- **Alberta:** Enduring Power of Attorney
- **British Columbia:** Enduring Power of Attorney
- **Saskatchewan:** Enduring Power of Attorney (under the POA Act)
- **Manitoba:** Enduring Power of Attorney
- **Quebec:** Mandate in case of incapacity (a distinct civil-law regime, must be homologated by a court)
- **Atlantic provinces:** Enduring Power of Attorney (each with its own act)

## What it covers

- **Banking:** accessing your accounts, paying your bills, managing your mortgage
- **Investments:** managing your portfolio, making buy/sell decisions
- **Real estate:** selling or refinancing property if needed to fund your care
- **Tax filing:** dealing with the CRA
- **Business:** managing your business interests, signing contracts
- **Government benefits:** applying for or managing CPP, OAS, disability, veterans' benefits

You can grant broad authority or narrow it down. Most people use broad enduring POAs so their attorney isn't hitting walls at the moment they most need to move.

## The court alternative — trusteeship / guardianship

Without a valid Continuing/Enduring POA, your family's only path to legal authority over your finances is a court-supervised **trusteeship** (Ontario) or **guardianship of property** (most other provinces).

This is not fast. It's not cheap. It's not private. And once it's set up, your trustee has to file annual accounts with the court, get court permission for major decisions, and pay ongoing legal fees for as long as the arrangement lasts.

Typical costs:
- Initial application and hearing: **$5,000–$15,000** in legal fees
- Ongoing court supervision: **$2,000–$5,000/year**
- Contested applications (where family members disagree about who should serve): **easily $50,000+**

Compare to the **$200–$500** cost of a Continuing/Enduring POA prepared in advance.

## Quebec is different — the Mandate

Quebec's civil-law regime doesn't use the common-law POA structure. Instead, Quebec residents prepare a **mandate given in anticipation of the mandator's incapacity** ("mandat de protection"). This document takes effect only after being homologated (court-approved) upon a finding of incapacity, which adds a layer but provides court oversight without a full guardianship.

If you live in Quebec: work with a notary. This is not a document to download from a template site.

## Who to name — and the second choice matters

You need to think about two people, not one.

**Your primary attorney** should be someone you trust completely with your money, who lives close enough to actually help, who is organized enough to keep records, and who is younger or healthier than you. Most people pick a spouse first.

**Your alternate attorney** takes over if your primary can't serve — because they've died, moved away, gotten sick themselves, or declined the role. Naming an alternate is not optional; without one, if your primary can't serve, your POA effectively fails and your family is back to the trusteeship application.

## What POAs *don't* cover

A POA for property covers financial decisions. It does **not** cover healthcare and personal-care decisions — you need a separate **POA for Personal Care** (Ontario), **Personal Directive** (Alberta), **Representation Agreement** (BC), or the local equivalent for those. Never assume one document covers both. Most well-drafted estate planning packages include both.

## What to do this week

If you don't have a Continuing/Enduring POA, this is the second-most important estate planning document in your life — arguably more important than your will if you're at any material risk of stroke, cognitive decline, or serious accident. Prices are low. The alternative is punishing for your family.

If you already have one, check the date. POAs older than 10 years are sometimes rejected by financial institutions even where technically still valid. A refresh every 5 years is a good idea.
