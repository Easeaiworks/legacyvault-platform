The will you spent $2,000 drafting doesn't touch the biggest assets you own. That's not a bug — it's how Canadian estate law works, and almost nobody knows.

## What beneficiary designations override

Any registered account or insurance contract with a named beneficiary passes directly to that person the moment you die. Your will has no authority over it (with one Quebec exception noted below). That means:

- **RRSP, RRIF, LIRA, LIF** — the plan document controls, not your will.
- **TFSA** — the successor holder / beneficiary designation controls.
- **RESP** — the subscriber's directions control; joint subscribers have survivorship rules.
- **Group life insurance and individual life policies** — the insurance contract controls.
- **Pension survivor benefits** — the pension plan document controls, often with mandatory spousal beneficiary rules for the pension portion.
- **Segregated funds** — the segregated fund contract controls (with creditor protection similar to insurance).

For most Canadians, these accounts hold more wealth than everything else combined. And they almost all pass outside your will.

## The five rules almost nobody knows

**Rule 1: In Quebec, informal beneficiary designations on RRSPs/TFSAs may be invalid.** Quebec generally requires beneficiary designations for registered plans to be made in a will or a notarized document — a form filled out at the bank is often *not enough*. If you're in Quebec, confirm with your notary that every designation is legally valid; otherwise the account falls into the estate and pays deemed-disposition tax immediately.

**Rule 2: Marriage does not automatically update your beneficiaries.** Getting married in any province doesn't remove your ex — or your parents, or a former partner — from any form. Every account needs to be updated by hand.

**Rule 3: Divorce or separation may or may not revoke.** Rules vary by province. In Ontario, section 52 of the Succession Law Reform Act revokes designations in favour of a former spouse in a will, but designations on the plan document itself may still be effective. In BC, the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA) has different rules. Assume nothing — update the paperwork.

**Rule 4: Naming your estate loses the spousal rollover.** If you leave a registered account payable to "my estate," the full balance is deemed disposed at death and taxed as income at your terminal rate — often 50%+. Naming your spouse (or a financially dependent minor or disabled child) directly preserves the tax-deferred rollover.

**Rule 5: Naming a minor as beneficiary triggers Public Trustee involvement.** If a 10-year-old inherits an RRIF or insurance benefit directly, the Public Guardian and Trustee (or equivalent provincial office) manages it until age of majority. Consider naming a trust or the parent's estate as beneficiary with clear terms instead.

## What to actually do

Set a recurring calendar reminder — once a year, and after any major life event:

1. Pull the beneficiary designation on every RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, RESP, LIRA, pension, group life certificate, and personal insurance policy.
2. Confirm both a **primary** and a **contingent** beneficiary are named.
3. If you're in Quebec, confirm with a notary that each designation is legally valid under Quebec civil law.
4. For beneficiaries who are minors, disabled, or vulnerable, talk to an estate lawyer about naming a trust rather than the person directly.
5. Store a signed copy of every current form with your estate documents.

Twenty minutes a year. It's the highest-leverage estate planning you can do.
