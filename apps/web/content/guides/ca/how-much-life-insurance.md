The industry rule of thumb — **10–12× your annual income** — is a decent starting point but leaves too much to chance for most families. The DIME method is the standard framework Canadian estate lawyers, financial planners, and honest insurance advisors use to size coverage properly.

## DIME

**D — Debt.** Everything you owe that would need to be paid off or serviced from your estate:
- Mortgage balance
- Vehicle loans
- Student debt (federally-issued in Canada is discharged at death; provincial and private vary)
- Credit card and line of credit balances
- Business debts you've personally guaranteed
- **RRSP/RRIF tax liability at death** if there's no spousal rollover — this can be substantial and is often overlooked

Add these up. That's your D.

**I — Income.** Multiply your annual after-tax income by how many years your family would need to replace it. For someone with young children, this is usually **10–15 years** — enough time for the surviving spouse to establish a career (or reestablish one) and for kids to reach age of independence.

Formula: (annual after-tax income) × (years of replacement) = your I.

**M — Mortgage.** If you counted mortgage under D, don't double-count here. Some versions of DIME break mortgage out separately to force families to think about whether they want the death benefit to pay it off outright (recommended in most cases) or continue paying it monthly.

**E — Education.** Estimated cost of the children's post-secondary education:
- Public university, in-province, living at home: $60,000–$90,000 per child (tuition + books)
- Public university, living away: $100,000–$140,000 per child
- Private university (rare in Canada, but relevant for US or UK plans): $250,000+
- If you're planning ahead 15–18 years, inflate at 4–5% annually

If you have RESPs, subtract projected RESP value at their age 18. But be honest: most families are under-contributing to RESPs.

## A real calculation

For our marketing director from the scenario:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| **Debt** (mortgage $620k, car loan $22k, LOC $10k) | $652,000 |
| **RRSP tax liability at death** (no spousal rollover option) | $130,000 |
| **Income** ($120k after-tax × 12 years) | $1,440,000 |
| **Education** ($100k × 3 kids in-province) | $300,000 |
| Subtract existing coverage, RESPs, and liquid assets | −$500,000 |
| **Recommended coverage** | **$2,022,000** |

You'd round up to $2M in a single policy or split across two policies for underwriting flexibility. $2M of 20-year term for a healthy 39-year-old non-smoker runs about **$85–$140 per month**.

## Term vs. permanent

**Term life** covers you for a specified period (10, 20, 30 years) and pays out only if you die during the term. It's the right answer for **90%+ of Canadian families**. Cheap, straightforward, sized for the years of highest financial risk (mortgage, young kids, peak earning years).

**Whole life / permanent life** covers you for your entire life and includes a cash value component. It costs **5–10× as much** as equivalent term coverage. Appropriate in narrow situations:

- Funding capital gains tax on death for high-net-worth families
- Equalizing inheritances when a family business goes to one child
- Long-term charitable giving structures
- Corporate-owned life insurance for shareholders of private companies

If someone is pitching you whole life without one of the above being your situation, get a second opinion from a fee-only advisor.

## Length of term

**T20 (20-year term)** is the most common and usually right for parents of young children. It gets you through mortgage payoff and kids-to-adulthood.

**T30 (30-year term)** costs a bit more but takes you further. Worth it if you're starting a family later.

**T10 (10-year term)** is cheap now but you'll be older when it expires — and re-underwriting at 45 or 50 costs more than the extra you saved.

## Insurance regulation and product landscape in Canada

Canadian life insurance is regulated federally by **OSFI** for capital adequacy and provincially for market conduct. Major carriers include Manulife, Sun Life, Canada Life, Empire Life, iA Financial, RBC Insurance, Desjardins, and several others. Any licensed carrier will pay a valid claim — brand name doesn't matter for coverage security.

**Use an independent broker,** not a captive agent from one company. An independent broker shops your health profile across the major carriers and finds the one that underwrites your specific situation most favorably. Brokers are paid by the insurance company — no cost to you — and can save you 30–50% versus the first quote.

Common brokerage-network names: PolicyMe (online-focused), PolicyAdvisor, InsuranceHotline, and a range of local independent brokers.

## Group insurance top-up

If your employer offers group life insurance, take it — usually 1–2× salary at very low cost. Then buy term privately to fill the gap between your group coverage and your DIME need.

Group insurance ends when you leave the job, so it should never be your only coverage.

## When to reevaluate

- **Every major life event:** marriage, birth of a child, home purchase, divorce, business ownership change
- **Every 5 years otherwise.** As your net worth grows and your mortgage shrinks, your coverage need may decrease
- **Before the term expires.** If your original T20 is running out and your kids are 10 and 12, you probably need to renew (at your then-current age, so it'll cost more)

## What to do this week

If you have no life insurance and dependents, get 3 quotes for the DIME-recommended amount from an independent broker. Apply for the best fit — the application process takes 4–8 weeks and includes a medical exam.

If you already have coverage, log it into your vault so your family knows it exists, and check that:

- The beneficiary is your **current spouse or common-law partner** (not your ex from 15 years ago)
- The contingent beneficiary is named (in case the primary predeceases you)
- The amount still reflects your family's actual need
- **RRSP/RRIF beneficiaries name your spouse, not "my estate"** — see our [single-income guide](/guides/single-income-household-plan) for why this matters
