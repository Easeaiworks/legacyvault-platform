Single-income households — where one adult earns and the other manages the home, cares for children, or is currently unable to work — face a distinct estate-planning challenge. The family's financial survival depends on protecting exactly one paycheque.

Most of the standard advice ("save more," "diversify," "delay CPP") applies to you too. But there are five things you have to get right that dual-income families can afford to be less careful about.

## 1. Life insurance sized to the actual replacement cost

Group life insurance through work — usually 1–2× salary — is nowhere near enough for a single-income household.

**Rough sizing:** aim for **10–12× the earner's annual income** in term life insurance. For a $100,000 earner, that's $1M–$1.2M of coverage. Sounds like a lot; it isn't, once you look at what it actually replaces.

- Mortgage payoff: often $300,000–$700,000 for a family home in most Canadian cities
- Kids to age 22 (living expenses + post-secondary): $200,000–$400,000
- Spouse's income replacement (5–10 years until they can retrain and re-enter the workforce): $250,000–$500,000
- Funeral, taxes on RRSP/RRIF at death, immediate emergency reserves: $50,000–$150,000

**The good news:** term life insurance is cheap. A healthy 35-year-old male non-smoker can get $1M of 20-year term for **$50–$80 per month**. Women pay less. Whole life / permanent policies cost 5–10× as much for the same coverage — for most single-income families, term is the right answer.

**Also consider group RRSP + Employer top-up.** Employer-sponsored group life insurance often covers 1–2× salary at almost no cost. Take it. Then buy term privately to fill the gap.

## 2. Disability insurance is arguably more important

Statistically, the single earner is much more likely to become disabled than to die during working years. Statistics Canada estimates about **1 in 4 workers** will experience a disability lasting more than 90 days at some point in their career.

CPP Disability is real but hard to qualify for, slow to approve, and pays modestly (average benefit ~$1,200/month). If your household depends on one paycheque, private long-term disability insurance is essential.

**Rough sizing:** enough to replace **60–70% of the earner's after-tax income** to age 65. Short-term disability is often included in employer group benefits; long-term disability sometimes is but frequently needs a top-up policy purchased separately. Cost is typically **1%–3% of income covered**.

## 3. Both spouses on the accounts

In a single-income household, the non-earner is often not named on the family's financial accounts. This creates real problems in an emergency:

- If the earning spouse becomes incapacitated, the non-earner can't access the money (see our [Power of Attorney for Property guide](/guides/power-of-attorney-property))
- If the earning spouse dies, everything is frozen pending probate
- If the household's credit cards are all in the earning spouse's name, the non-earning spouse has no independent credit history — a serious problem if they need to re-enter the workforce or apply for a mortgage on their own

**Concrete actions:**

- Add the non-earning spouse as a **joint owner** on at least one chequing account, one savings account, and one credit card
- Make sure the non-earning spouse has their own credit history — a credit card in their name only, used and paid off, is enough

## 4. Spousal RRSP + TFSA for the non-earning spouse

Canada has two structural tools that matter a lot for single-income households:

**Spousal RRSP.** The earning spouse contributes to an RRSP registered in the non-earning spouse's name. The earner gets the tax deduction; the account belongs to (and grows in) the non-earner. This shifts retirement assets to the non-earner and can dramatically reduce combined tax in retirement. Look up the specific contribution rules — the contributing spouse's deduction limit applies, but the withdrawal rules attach to the annuitant.

**TFSA.** Every Canadian adult accumulates TFSA room whether they're earning or not. The non-earning spouse builds room every year even without any income. The working spouse can gift money to the non-working spouse specifically for TFSA contributions without CRA attribution rules kicking in (unlike gifts to a spouse for non-registered investing).

**Both together:** the non-earning spouse should have a Spousal RRSP for large tax-advantaged retirement savings *and* their own TFSA for tax-free growth. Both are built during the earner's peak years.

## 5. The beneficiary audit

Every account with a beneficiary designation should name the current spouse first, contingent beneficiaries second, and never "my estate."

For **RRSPs and RRIFs**, naming the spouse (or common-law partner) as beneficiary allows a **tax-deferred rollover** into the surviving spouse's RRSP or RRIF. Naming anyone else — even an adult child — triggers full taxation of the entire RRSP/RRIF value in the year of death.

On a $500,000 RRSP with a non-spouse beneficiary, that can mean **$150,000–$250,000 in tax** the estate has to pay before the remainder is distributed. If the deceased didn't have that liquidity outside the RRSP, the estate has to sell other assets to cover it.

For **TFSAs**, naming your spouse as **successor holder** (not just beneficiary) allows the TFSA to be transferred to their TFSA tax-free with no impact on their contribution room. This is different from naming them as beneficiary, which counts against their room.

Do the audit today. Log into every account (RRSP, RRIF, TFSA, life insurance, pension, non-registered). Look at who's listed. Update anything that's wrong.

## Provincial callout: common-law partners

In most provinces, common-law partners have **no rights under intestacy law** — if you die without a will, your common-law partner inherits nothing from the estate. **British Columbia** is the exception (2+ year cohabitation grants married-equivalent rights).

If your household is single-income and common-law, this changes everything. See our [why-you-need-a-will guide](/guides/why-you-need-a-will) for more, and consider whether making the relationship legally recognized (or having an ironclad will) is the right protection.

## The order of operations

If you're starting from zero:

1. **Term life insurance** — the biggest single lift for the least money
2. **Employer's group LTD** if available; supplement if needed
3. **Beneficiary audit** — 30 minutes of your life. RRSP rollover to spouse. TFSA successor holder.
4. **Emergency fund** — build to 3 months of expenses fast, then 6 months
5. **Non-earning spouse on accounts** — joint chequing, joint credit card, independent credit
6. **Spousal RRSP + TFSA for the non-earner** — start contributing now
7. **Will + POA + healthcare directive** — the essentials (see our other guides)
8. **If common-law:** consider marriage or execute an airtight will and cohabitation agreement

If you do steps 1–3 this week, you've meaningfully reduced the single largest financial risk your family faces.
