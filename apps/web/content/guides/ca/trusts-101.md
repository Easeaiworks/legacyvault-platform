A trust is a container. You put assets into it. You name someone (a *trustee*) to manage what's inside, and you write instructions for how it's managed and who eventually gets what. In Canada, trusts also carry specific tax consequences — good and bad — that make them powerful when used correctly and expensive when misused.

## The two big Canadian trust use cases

**Bypass probate on real estate.** Provinces vary widely on probate fees. Ontario charges roughly 1.5% on estate value above $50,000 — meaningful money on a $2M estate. Nova Scotia and BC also have material probate fees. Assets held in a trust are not part of the probate estate. For a cottage or a second property, this can save tens of thousands.

**Multiply the lifetime capital gains exemption ($1.25M in 2024).** A family trust holding qualified small business corporation shares can allow each beneficiary to claim their own exemption on eventual sale — potentially saving hundreds of thousands in tax. Requires careful setup and ongoing compliance.

## The four Canadian trusts you'll hear about

**Alter-ego trust (settlor age 65+).** You transfer assets to a trust you control alone; income and capital are yours during life; at your death, assets pass to named beneficiaries outside probate. Because you get all the income during life, the transfer to the trust does NOT trigger a deemed disposition at the time of transfer. Popular for real estate and non-registered investments. Cannot hold registered accounts.

**Joint-partner trust (settlor age 65+ with a spouse or common-law partner).** Same as alter-ego but for a couple. Assets pass to survivor first, then to remaining beneficiaries at second death — all outside probate.

**Testamentary trust.** Created in your will and funded at death. Historically had major tax advantages (graduated rates) but 2016 rules restricted this to 36 months post-death for most trusts. Still useful for managing inheritances for minors or vulnerable beneficiaries.

**Family (inter vivos) trust.** Set up during life for estate freezing and income-splitting purposes. Common in business succession planning. The 21-year rule triggers deemed disposition of all trust assets every 21 years — build that into the plan.

## When you probably don't need a trust

- Your estate is straightforward — most assets flow to a spouse via joint tenancy and beneficiary designations on RRSPs / RRIFs / TFSAs / life insurance (all of which bypass probate anyway).
- You live in a province with negligible probate fees (Quebec has none; Alberta caps at $525).
- You're under 65 and don't have a specific tax-planning or family-protection need.

## When a trust is probably worth it

- You own a cottage, vacation property, or investment real estate — especially in Ontario, BC, or the Maritimes where probate fees bite.
- You're 65+ and want to simplify what happens at death.
- You have a family business and want to freeze the current value for estate tax purposes while future growth accrues to the next generation.
- You have a disabled beneficiary who receives ODSP, AISH, or equivalent provincial disability benefits — a Henson trust can preserve those benefits.
- You're in a blended family and want to provide for your current partner without disinheriting children from a prior relationship.

## Quebec is different

Quebec's civil law system treats trusts differently from common-law provinces. Trusts in Quebec are creatures of the Civil Code and require careful drafting by a Quebec notary. Many US and other-province trust structures don't translate cleanly. If you're a Quebec resident, work with a Quebec-licensed notary or estate lawyer.

## The most important warning

Trusts have annual filing requirements. Every trust in Canada must file a T3 return (with new bare-trust reporting rules that came into force in 2024). Setup is one-time; compliance is forever. Factor in the accountant's fee — typically $500–$1,500 per year — before deciding a trust is right for you.
