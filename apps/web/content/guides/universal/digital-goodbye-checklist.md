The digital piece of an estate is the newest and worst-documented. It's also where LegacyVault does its most concrete work.

Here's the complete checklist. If your executor can find this list, along with the right instructions for each item, your family avoids months of small nightmares.

## 1. Devices

**Phone(s).**
- Model and current iOS/Android version
- Passcode or unlock method — write it down in a sealed envelope with your will, or store in a password manager whose emergency access is set up (see §6)
- Apple ID or Google account associated with the phone
- If Face ID / fingerprint unlock — a passcode still works, that's the fallback

**Computer(s).**
- Login password
- Disk encryption recovery key (FileVault on Mac, BitLocker on Windows) — this is the killer if you don't have it
- Any other user accounts on the machine

**Smart-home devices.**
- Router admin password
- Wi-Fi password
- Smart lock backup access codes
- Ring / Nest / Arlo account passwords
- Alarm system codes and provider contact

## 2. Password manager

If you use one (and you should):
- Which service (1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane, Apple Keychain, etc.)
- Vault Password location — never in the same place as the vault itself
- Recovery code — printed, stored with your will
- Emergency Access / Family Sharing enabled for a trusted person if the service supports it

**LegacyVault's Credential Vault** (coming soon) offers zero-knowledge storage of credentials and security-question answers with Shamir secret-share recovery for executors. Until then, using an established password manager with its own family/emergency access feature is the strongest option.

## 3. Two-factor authentication

This is the one people forget and it locks families out completely.

- Which authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy, 1Password's built-in, etc.)
- **Seed phrases / backup codes** for each account with 2FA — every service that provides 2FA also provides recovery codes when you set it up. Save them.
- SIM-based 2FA (SMS codes) — depends on your phone number staying active

If your family can unlock your phone and password manager, they can access 2FA codes. If they can't unlock either, the 2FA codes may be permanently unreachable.

## 4. Cloud storage & photos

- iCloud, Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive
- Photo services (Apple Photos, Google Photos, Amazon Photos, Flickr)
- Note whether photos are shared with family members already (family sharing preserves access)
- **Print or export the important ones now**, don't rely on cloud alone

## 5. Financial and cryptocurrency accounts

Beyond your regular bank and brokerage accounts:

- Cryptocurrency exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini, Wealthsimple Crypto, etc.) — usernames and 2FA method
- **Hardware wallets** (Ledger, Trezor) — location of the device *and* location of the seed phrase (in different secure locations)
- **Never share the seed phrase directly**, but document where it can be found — with your will, with your executor, in a bank safe deposit box
- Payment services (PayPal, Venmo, Cash App, Wise, Zelle)
- Merchant credits and gift-card balances

**Cryptocurrency without a recovery path is functionally lost.** This is unique to crypto — traditional bank accounts can be accessed by a court-appointed executor with a death certificate; crypto cannot.

## 6. Subscriptions

Every recurring charge:

- Streaming (Netflix, Spotify, Apple Music, Disney+, HBO Max, YouTube Premium, etc.)
- Software (Adobe, Microsoft 365, iCloud, Google One, Dropbox, 1Password)
- News & content (NYT, WSJ, Substacks, magazine subscriptions)
- Meal kits, subscription boxes, product-of-the-month clubs
- Gym & apps (Peloton, ClassPass, meditation apps)
- Domain names and web hosting

For each: **provider name, subscription level, billing amount, billing method (which card), and your preference: cancel immediately, transfer to spouse, or maintain.**

## 7. Social media

Each platform has different handling on death:

- **Facebook** — can be memorialized (kept live but marked) or deleted. You can appoint a **Legacy Contact** in advance who can request either after your death.
- **Instagram** — memorialization or deletion, similar to Facebook
- **X (formerly Twitter)** — can be deactivated after death by an authorized family member
- **LinkedIn** — memorialization or removal
- **Snapchat, TikTok, Reddit** — deletion possible, no memorialization
- **YouTube** — inactive account manager can transfer or delete after 3–18 months of inactivity

**Set up in-advance controls where possible.** Facebook and Google both let you designate a legacy/inactive contact today. Do it.

## 8. Email accounts

- Personal Gmail / Outlook / Apple Mail
- Any work-related email you own
- Email forwarding rules (turn them off; they can leak information)
- Downloaded archives — an .mbox or PST export saves the historical correspondence even after the account closes

Google's **Inactive Account Manager** lets you designate someone to receive access after N months of inactivity. This is the single most important digital-life setting most people never touch.

## 9. Pet care

Not digital, but often forgotten:

- Vet contact and preferred care plan
- Medications and dosing
- Feeding schedule and food brand
- Emergency contact person willing to take the pet
- Insurance policy if applicable

## 10. Household quirks

Small stuff that costs families weeks of confusion:

- Water shutoff valve location
- Circuit breaker labeling
- Alarm codes
- Where you keep spare keys (and who else has them)
- Thermostat quirks and preferred settings
- Property tax and utility account numbers
- HOA / condo fee arrangements

## The maintenance discipline

The digital-goodbye document goes stale fast. New account every month. Old account cancelled. Password rotated. New device.

**Set a recurring reminder to review this list every 6 months.** LegacyVault's Digital Goodbye module (already shipped) does this for you — every entry has a `lastReviewedAt` and the dashboard flags anything not reviewed in a year.

## The most important habit

**Never write down a password on paper that's kept in the same location as the device it unlocks.** Sticky note under the keyboard is worse than no password at all — it fails on the burglary use case *and* the death use case.

Instead: use a password manager. Document the recovery method for the password manager separately. Trust the system, not the individual notes.
