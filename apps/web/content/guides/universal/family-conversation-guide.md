If you're doing the work of an estate plan, you're already ahead of most people. But the plan itself is only half the job. The other half is telling the people affected — not the mechanics of the plan, but the *reasons*. Because after you're gone, the reasons are the one thing that can't be reconstructed from a document.

This guide is a structured way to have that conversation. It's not a script. It's a scaffold.

## Who to include

**Definitely include:** your spouse or partner; your adult children (each of them, individually if the situations differ); anyone you've named as executor or trustee; anyone whose inheritance is materially different from what they might expect.

**Optionally include:** the parents you're caring for; adult siblings who play significant roles in family logistics; step-children; long-term friends or godchildren who appear in the plan.

**Do not skip anyone whose inheritance is smaller than they might expect.** The most damaging surprises are the ones that feel like exclusions — even when there's a good reason.

## Two settings that work

**One-on-one, over a meal.** Slower, more intimate, more room for the person to react. Best for anyone whose situation is unusual — a child who won't inherit equally, a spouse in a second marriage, an executor being handed a heavy job. Plan for at least two hours. Turn off phones.

**Family meeting, everyone at once.** Better for consistency and for defusing later "what did they say to you separately?" dynamics. Best held when the plan is symmetrical across children, or when you want to introduce a professional (estate lawyer, financial planner, or family mediator) as part of the discussion. Plan for a Saturday afternoon.

## The five things to actually say

**1. "I have a plan, and I want you to know what's in it."** Start with the fact that a plan exists. Many family members are relieved just to hear this — they've been anxious about it and haven't known how to ask.

**2. "Here's what happens at my death, in order."** Walk through it: who is executor, who has power of attorney, where the will is, what the primary bequests are. Keep it factual for the first pass. Emotions will come later; don't rush past the facts.

**3. "Here's the reasoning behind the parts that are different."** This is the hardest and most valuable part. If one child gets the family business and another gets cash, explain why. If a stepchild is treated differently from biological children, explain why. If a spouse in a second marriage has a life-interest arrangement rather than outright inheritance, explain why. The reasoning is what protects relationships after you're gone.

**4. "Here's what I hope for after I'm gone."** Not legally binding, but emotionally central. What do you hope the family looks like in five years? Ten? What are your wishes about specific heirlooms, family traditions, or the family gathering places? Say it out loud. Write some of it down.

**5. "What questions do you have?"** Then be quiet. Long silences are okay. People often need time to formulate what they actually want to ask.

## The questions to be ready for

**"Why did you choose them as executor instead of me?"** Have a real answer. Usually it's about proximity, professional skill, or family peace — not love or trust.

**"How much is it, actually?"** Some families want the number. Others don't. Follow the questioner's lead; if they ask, tell them honestly.

**"Have you talked to my brother/sister/step-sibling about this?"** Yes, and here's what I said to them. Consistency prevents future conflict.

**"What if I disagree with something?"** Listen. You can hear feedback and still decide to keep the plan as-is. But hearing it now, while you can respond, is a gift both ways.

## The one thing not to promise

Do not promise a specific dollar amount, a specific asset, or a specific outcome. Life happens between now and your death. Health events, real estate, inflation, market crashes, second marriages, businesses that fail or explode — any of these can materially change what's actually available at death. Speak about *shares* and *proportions*, not exact numbers.

## After the conversation

Follow up in writing. A short letter, an email, or a note attached to your estate documents saying: "On [date], I spoke with [name] about the plan. We discussed [main points]. They asked about [X]." This creates a record that helps everyone remember, protects the plan from later challenge, and — if you record a legacy message in your vault — gives you a foundation to build on.

The people you love deserve to hear this from you, in your own voice, while you can still explain. Do it this year.
