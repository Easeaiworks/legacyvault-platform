Blended families face an estate planning problem that a single "everything to my spouse" will can never solve: how do you make sure your current partner is provided for during their remaining lifetime, *and* that your children from an earlier relationship eventually inherit what you meant for them to inherit? The default of "everything to spouse" almost always fails the second half of that promise, because your surviving spouse writes their own will after you're gone — and there is nothing legally binding them to remember your children.

## The four honest conversations

Blended-family estate planning starts with conversations most families never have out loud.

**With your spouse.** What does "our" money mean, and what does "yours" and "mine" mean? Which assets came into the marriage from your side? Which from theirs? What are each of your obligations to children from prior relationships? Are you comfortable making those obligations explicit and legally binding?

**With your children.** Do they know what to expect? Do they know your spouse is not their enemy in your estate plan? Do they know the structure — that they may inherit later, after your spouse — rather than assuming they've been forgotten?

**With your ex, if you're co-parenting.** Are you both funding education, life insurance, or accounts for the children? What do your respective wills say about the children's inheritance?

**With your lawyer.** They've seen every version of this. They know which structures work and which fall apart.

## The three tools that actually solve this

**A QTIP trust (US) or life-interest / spousal trust (Canada) with children as remainder beneficiaries.** You leave assets in a trust that pays your surviving spouse income (and sometimes principal) for their lifetime. When your spouse dies, whatever's left goes to your children — not to your spouse's estate or their new spouse. This is the single most common blended-family solution. Requires an attorney to structure correctly.

**Separate life insurance for the children.** Buy a life insurance policy naming your children directly as beneficiaries. This is money that goes to them, no matter what your spouse does with the rest. In many blended families, this is the "peace of mind" that lets the couple leave the primary estate to the survivor without guilt.

**Prenuptial or postnuptial agreement / marriage contract.** Not romantic, but real. Documents what's yours, what's theirs, what's shared, and what happens to each pot at death or divorce. Enforceable in most jurisdictions if properly drafted. Especially important where the wealth disparity between partners is significant, or where either partner brought substantial pre-marriage assets.

## The four biggest pitfalls

**Leaving everything to your spouse and hoping.** As above — your spouse's will controls everything after your death. Your promise to your children is only as good as your spouse's willingness to remember it in a document you can no longer influence.

**Naming your spouse and your children as joint beneficiaries in equal shares.** Simple math: your 62-year-old spouse and your three 30-something children each get 25%. Your spouse now has a fraction of the resources they may need for a 30-year retirement. Nobody is happy.

**Forgetting to update beneficiary designations after divorce and remarriage.** Retirement accounts, life insurance, and TOD accounts pass by designation — not by will. If your ex-spouse is still listed, they inherit, regardless of what your will or your current marriage says.

**Naming your children as beneficiaries of a house your current spouse lives in.** The children inherit the house immediately at your death. Your spouse now has a lifetime tenant arrangement with your children as landlords — a recipe for lawsuits and cut-off relationships. Use a life-estate or a trust structure instead.

## Where to start

Book a two-hour consultation with an estate lawyer who explicitly does blended-family work. Bring your current will, a list of assets, a list of beneficiary designations, and — most importantly — bring your spouse. This is a plan you make together, not a plan you make alone and then break the news about.
