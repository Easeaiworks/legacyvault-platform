Hiring an estate lawyer isn't a mystery. It's not gambling. You're buying a service, and there's a right way and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way costs more, produces worse results, and leaves you feeling like you got taken advantage of. The right way costs less, produces better results, and leaves you knowing what you got and why.

Here's the whole thing.

## Who you actually need

Not every legal situation needs an estate lawyer. Sort by complexity:

**Simple situation** (single or married, straightforward assets under ~$500k, no business ownership, no blended family, no special-needs dependents): an online will service (Willful, Trust & Will, FreeWill, Rocket Lawyer) or a general-practice attorney charging **$300–$800** for a will + POA + healthcare directive package is fine.

**Moderate complexity** (assets $500k–$3M, real estate in multiple states/provinces, a small business or rental property, adult children with different needs, second marriage): you need a **specialist estate planning attorney**. Expect **$1,500–$5,000** for a comprehensive plan.

**High complexity** (assets over $3M, private business ownership, disabled dependent, cross-border assets, potential federal estate tax exposure in the US, complex family dynamics): you need a specialist with **TEP (Trust and Estate Practitioner)** or equivalent credentials and often experience with your specific issue. Expect **$5,000–$20,000+** for a plan; ongoing relationship fees on top of that.

**Ongoing administration** (you've been named executor of someone else's estate): often billed hourly, **$250–$650/hour** depending on region and firm size. Contested estates run far higher.

## How to find candidates

Two reliable sources:

**Bar / law society referral services.** Every state bar or provincial law society runs a lawyer-referral service — you call, describe your issue, and they connect you to 2–3 lawyers who match. These lawyers are all in good standing (bar-verified). Cost is usually a modest consultation fee ($40–$100 for a 30-minute meeting). This is the most reliable low-cost starting point.

**Peer networks.** Ask your accountant, your financial planner, or the lawyer who handled your real estate transaction who they use personally. Professionals who deal with estate lawyers regularly know who's competent and who's not. This is often the highest-signal source.

Google Reviews and Yelp are not useless but not enough on their own. Look at the pattern of complaints, not any individual review.

## What to ask in the first phone call

Before you meet anyone in person, do a 10-minute phone screen with 2–3 candidates. Skip anyone who won't do this.

Six questions:

1. **"What percentage of your practice is estate planning?"** You want 50%+ for a specialist. 100% is fine and often better.
2. **"How do you charge?"** Flat fee (most common for planning), hourly (common for administration), retainer (common for complex ongoing work), or contingency (rare in estate work; avoid it here).
3. **"What's included in your flat fee for a will + POA + healthcare directive package?"** Get the itemized answer. Are draft revisions included? A follow-up meeting?
4. **"What's your policy on questions after the plan is done?"** A lawyer who charges hourly to answer a 5-minute email 6 months later is a lawyer whose plan you'll never revisit.
5. **"Have you handled a case like mine?"** Describe the actual complexity — blended family, disabled child, business ownership, out-of-state real estate.
6. **"How long from now to signed documents?"** 4–8 weeks is normal. 6+ months usually means either a bottleneck at their firm or you're being deprioritized.

## Warnings that should end the conversation

- **Won't quote a flat fee up front for a standard plan.** Standard estate plans are commodities. If the lawyer can't tell you the price for the standard version, they're not doing many of them.
- **Immediate hard sell for a trust when a will would suffice.** Trusts are the right tool for specific situations (avoiding probate on real estate, providing for a minor or disabled beneficiary, privacy). If a lawyer recommends a trust in the first 15 minutes without asking about your assets, family, or goals, you're being upsold.
- **Vague about their specialty and experience.** "Oh, I do a bit of everything" is not an answer.
- **Won't put credentials on their website.** Real estate lawyers list their credentials, bar admission, and specialty designations publicly.
- **Won't let you review the documents before signing.** Any lawyer worth hiring will send drafts for review before the signing meeting.

## What to bring to the first paid meeting

- List of your assets: real estate, retirement accounts, brokerage accounts, insurance policies, business interests
- Names and dates of birth for immediate family and anyone you'd want to include in your plan
- Copies of any existing documents (old wills, POAs, insurance policies with beneficiary designations)
- Questions you want answered — write them down; you'll forget in the moment
- A picture of your family situation, in your own words: who you're married to, kids from prior relationships, whom you want to receive what, whom you'd trust to handle things

## What the good plan looks like

For most people, a completed plan includes:

- **Last Will and Testament** — asset distribution, executor appointment, guardian nomination for minor kids
- **Financial Power of Attorney** — someone to manage your finances if you can't
- **Healthcare Power of Attorney + Living Will** (US) or **POA for Personal Care** (Canada, varies by province)
- **Beneficiary designation review** — the lawyer should ask about your retirement accounts and life insurance and either update them or tell you which forms to update yourself
- **Letter of instruction** — non-legal document with the practical details (funeral wishes, executor guidance, asset inventory location)
- **Signed originals in a safe location** with copies to your executor and your primary contacts

A comprehensive plan often adds:
- Revocable living trust (US) or Alter Ego / Joint Partner Trust (Canada, age 65+)
- Special-needs trust for disabled beneficiaries
- HIPAA authorization (US) or provincial equivalent
- Business succession documents

You get to see and understand every one of these before you sign. Ask questions until the answers make sense.

## The maintenance conversation

Life changes. Estate plans need reviewing every 3–5 years or after any major event (marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary, move to a different state/province, significant asset change).

Ask your lawyer at the outset: "What's your policy on future revisions?" Some firms include one revision per year in the original fee. Others charge hourly. Neither is wrong — you just need to know.

**Set a reminder in your calendar to revisit the plan every 3 years.** Not annually — that's overkill and you'll ignore it. Every 3 years is enough to catch drift.

## What to do this week

1. Make a list of the family and financial pieces you'd bring to a meeting.
2. Call two candidates from your state bar / law society referral list or LegacyVault's directory. Do the 10-minute phone screen.
3. Book the paid consultation with the one who feels honest and answered plainly.
4. Show up prepared. Ask your questions. Sign the plan.

Total time investment: about 6–8 hours over 6 weeks. Total cost, for most families: $500–$2,500. Value delivered to your family: incalculable.
