The first 30 days after a death sit inside the strangest window most people ever face. You're grieving, everyone around you is grieving, and you have a list of practical things that all need to happen fast — some within days, some within weeks. Missing them costs money, closes doors, and adds bureaucracy on top of grief.

If you're reading this because you've just been named executor: you don't need to do everything on this list today. But you do need to know what's on it.

## First 24 hours

**Confirm the death legally.** In a hospital or hospice, this happens automatically — you'll get a pronouncement of death. At home, call 911 if unexpected, or the hospice on-call number if under hospice care. You need this pronouncement before anything else can move.

**Contact the funeral home.** If preferences were documented (in a will, LegacyVault funeral-wishes module, or a separate document), follow them. If not, and you're overwhelmed, most funeral homes will walk you through it slowly.

**Notify immediate family.** Divide this up. Nobody has to make all the calls alone.

**Secure the residence.** Lock doors, take valuables out of visible sight, remove or forward any medications, feed and arrange care for pets. If the deceased lived alone, plan for at least one person to check the residence daily for the first two weeks — mail piles up and it's how burglars know a place is empty.

**Do not immediately publish the obituary or death on social media.** Wait 24–48 hours until you've secured the residence and notified close family. Publicly announced deaths sometimes trigger scam attempts and burglaries.

## First week

**Order 10–15 certified death certificates.** You'll need originals — not copies — for financial institutions, insurance companies, government agencies, and title transfers. Most funeral homes will handle the initial order; you can request more from your state/provincial vital records office later. **Cost: $10–$25 each.** Getting extra now is cheaper than reordering later.

**Locate the will.** Check LegacyVault, the deceased's home safe, safe deposit box (may need a court order to access if it's not jointly held), the attorney who drafted the will, and any storage they used. If you can't find one, follow intestate succession procedures.

**Contact the estate attorney.** Even if you're planning to handle much of the administration yourself, one meeting with the drafting attorney (if there is one) clarifies what needs court involvement.

**Notify the primary employer** if the deceased was working. HR will handle final pay, unused vacation, group life insurance, retirement account paperwork, and continuation-of-benefits notices.

**Contact Social Security / Canada Pension Plan.**
- **US:** Call SSA at 1-800-772-1213 or visit an office. If a funeral home is helping, they usually file the initial notice (Form SSA-721). Survivor benefits for spouses and dependent children start after this filing.
- **Canada:** Contact Service Canada (1-800-277-9914) to report the death for CPP and OAS. If the deceased was receiving benefits, you must return any payments issued after death. Death benefit and survivor benefit applications happen here.

**Notify life insurance companies.** Each will require a certified death certificate and a claim form. Term life claims typically pay within 30–60 days.

**Initial notice to banks and credit-card companies.** Not to close accounts yet — just to note the death and stop any autopay that shouldn't continue. Do NOT close credit cards immediately if there are automatic bills you need to redirect first.

**Freeze credit** with all three US bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) or the Canadian bureaus (Equifax and TransUnion Canada). Deceased identities are heavily targeted by fraudsters in the weeks following death.

## First 30 days

**Meet with the estate attorney** to plan probate (or the equivalent depending on jurisdiction). Bring:
- Certified death certificate
- Original will
- List of assets and where they are (LegacyVault estate binder is exactly this)
- List of debts
- List of beneficiaries and their contact information

**Open a probate estate bank account** if probate is happening. All estate income and payments flow through this account, keeping estate money separate from your personal funds.

**Notify all financial institutions of the death.** Each will require a certified death certificate and any account-specific forms. Understand which accounts pass automatically (joint accounts with survivorship, TOD/POD, retirement accounts with beneficiaries, life insurance) vs. which flow through probate.

**Notify insurance carriers** — health insurance, auto insurance (change registration/insurance if a vehicle passes to a beneficiary), homeowner's/renter's insurance, umbrella policies. Some policies pay out for accidental death; check.

**Cancel or transfer subscriptions.** Streaming services, memberships, magazines, meal kits. If LegacyVault Digital Goodbye was set up, this is where you follow that list. Cost of not doing this: sometimes hundreds of dollars a month for months.

**Notify credit-card companies formally** and close the accounts (after redirecting any autopays that need to continue).

**Contact utilities** (electric, gas, water, internet, phone) to either close, transfer to a survivor, or continue paying from the estate.

**Post-employment tasks.** COBRA / provincial equivalent decisions have short deadlines (60 days in the US, varies in Canada). Group life insurance conversion has a 30–60 day window.

**Update your own documents.** If you were named in the deceased's will or as a beneficiary elsewhere, your own estate documents may need updating.

## Financial and legal, first 90 days

**Real estate.** If the deceased owned a home:
- Maintain insurance — vacant-home insurance if it will be unoccupied
- Keep utilities on
- Determine whether it will be sold, transferred to an heir, or kept in the estate
- Property tax obligations continue

**Vehicles.** Titles need to be transferred (varies by state/province — some states allow small-estate transfers without probate).

**Business interests.** If the deceased owned or partly owned a business, there may be a buy-sell agreement triggered. Talk to the business's attorney and the surviving partners.

**Digital assets.** Follow the digital-goodbye list. Facebook memorial or delete. Google inactive-account manager. Cryptocurrency (this is where the hardware wallet + seed phrase decision matters most). Photo archives.

**Tax obligations.**
- **US:** Final personal tax return (Form 1040 for the year of death) plus potentially an estate income tax return (Form 1041) if the estate has income. Federal estate tax return (Form 706) only for estates over the federal exemption (~$14M in 2026, though this changes with legislation).
- **Canada:** Final personal tax return (T1) for the year of death. Deemed disposition rules trigger capital gains on most appreciated assets at death (with spousal rollover exceptions). RRSPs and RRIFs are fully taxable in the final year unless rolled to a spouse.

Consult a CPA or accountant for anything but the simplest estate. **The cost of getting the final tax return wrong is usually much more than the cost of the accountant.**

## What tends to be forgotten

- **Passwords and 2FA authenticators** — if the deceased didn't leave a documented digital-goodbye plan, this is the single most common source of prolonged frustration
- **Safe deposit boxes** — access rules vary; some require a court order
- **Old workplace retirement accounts** — pensions from previous jobs, forgotten 401(k)s, forgotten RRSPs
- **Airline miles, credit card points, hotel loyalty accounts** — usually transferable if you know they exist and act within the airline's time window
- **Unclaimed government benefits** — CPP death benefit, Social Security death benefit, veterans' benefits, tax refunds owed to the deceased
- **Ongoing subscriptions on the deceased's credit cards** — meal kits, gym memberships, streaming services, and less-obvious ones (app subscriptions on Apple/Google accounts)

## The emotional part

You're doing administrative work while grieving. Two things help:

**Break it up.** Aim for 1–2 hours a day of estate work. More than that is often counterproductive. The mail keeps coming; you'll catch up.

**Ask for help.** Adult children of the deceased, siblings, close friends — divide the tasks. Nobody has to do this alone. If the estate is at all complex, the attorney is worth it not just for legal expertise but for having someone else own the timeline.
