There are two numbers most people can casually save: something like $200 a month, and something like $0. The difference between them at the end of 40 years is the difference between a comfortable retirement and one that isn't.

## The table

Every dollar you can afford to move into a diversified low-cost investment does two jobs: it earns a return, and then that return earns a return, and then *that* return earns a return. Given enough time, the second and third effects dwarf the first.

Here's what regular monthly contributions become, assuming a **balanced 6% average annual return** (roughly what a broadly diversified index fund has done historically, though past performance guarantees nothing).

| Monthly | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years | 40 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $100 | $16,470 | $46,435 | $100,954 | $200,145 |
| $200 | $32,940 | $92,870 | $201,908 | $400,290 |
| $300 | $49,410 | $139,305 | $302,862 | $600,435 |
| $500 | $82,350 | $232,175 | $504,770 | $1,000,725 |
| $750 | $123,525 | $348,263 | $757,155 | $1,501,088 |
| $1,000 | $164,700 | $464,350 | $1,009,540 | $2,001,450 |

## What jumps out

**$200 a month for 30 years is over $200,000.** That's an above-the-line replacement for one full year of a typical income, or a substantial cushion for your family after your death.

**$500 a month for 40 years passes $1 million.** For a couple who each contribute $250/month in a tax-advantaged account (Roth IRA in the US, TFSA in Canada), that's a real retirement — not luxury, but freedom.

**The 40-year totals are more than double the 30-year totals** at every contribution level. Starting a decade earlier isn't a small advantage. It's the whole game.

## Three-scenario ranges

The 6% figure is the middle scenario. Historical averages for broadly diversified equity portfolios have run higher; bond-heavy portfolios lower. Here are three scenarios for the $200/month case:

| Scenario | 10 years | 20 years | 30 years | 40 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conservative** 4% | $29,443 | $73,353 | $138,824 | $236,568 |
| **Balanced** 6% | $32,940 | $92,870 | $201,908 | $400,290 |
| **Growth** 8% | $36,833 | $118,589 | $300,059 | $703,059 |

The right assumption depends on your allocation and your time horizon. For someone 30 years from retirement, an all-equity allocation historically returned closer to 8% real. For someone 5 years from retirement, a bond-heavy allocation is more like 4%.

**None of these are guarantees.** The next 40 years will not look exactly like the last 40. But the direction is clear.

## The account you use matters

Where you put the money determines whether you pay taxes on the growth.

**In the US:**
- **Roth IRA** — after-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals in retirement. Best-in-class for most young/middle-income earners. 2026 limit: $7,000/year ($8,000 if 50+).
- **401(k) with employer match** — get the match first, always. This is free money before we even talk about returns.
- **Traditional IRA / Traditional 401(k)** — tax deduction now, taxed later. Better if you expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement.
- **Taxable brokerage account** — no contribution limits, but you pay tax on dividends and gains. Fine for money above the tax-advantaged limits.

**In Canada:**
- **TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account)** — after-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals ever. Structurally similar to a Roth IRA and equally powerful. 2026 limit: $7,000/year.
- **RRSP** — tax deduction now, taxed on withdrawal. Best for higher earners who expect to be in a lower bracket in retirement.
- **Employer pension match** — same rule as the US 401(k) match: take the match first.
- **Non-registered account** — no contribution limits, capital gains taxed at 50% inclusion rate.

The specific account matters *less* than the fact that you're doing it consistently. The single biggest mistake at every income level: waiting until "we can afford more" to start.

## Where the estate connection is

If you die tomorrow, the compound growth in your accounts is what your family lives on until the estate settles — which in most jurisdictions is 6–18 months. A properly funded retirement account with a named beneficiary transfers **fast, tax-efficiently, and outside probate**. That's why the beneficiary designation on the account (see [our beneficiary guide](/guides/beneficiary-designations)) matters as much as the balance.

## What to do this week

If you're not currently saving anything, set up an automatic $50/month transfer to a Roth IRA (US) or TFSA (Canada) invested in a low-cost total-market index fund. That's it. That's the whole action item.

If you're already saving, look at whether you're getting your full employer match. If you're not, you're leaving money on the table every paycheck.

If you're maxing tax-advantaged accounts already, this guide isn't for you. But the person you love who isn't saving? Forward them this table.
